DNCC Dengue Watch

Automated Dengue Surveillance & Ward-Level Risk Intelligence

An automated data and analytics system developed for Dhaka North City Corporation (DNCC) to monitor dengue trends, identify high-risk wards and support targeted mosquito-control decisions.

Public Health
Dhaka North City Corporation (DNCC)
Live Dashboard
dnccinnovationlab.github.io/dengue-dashboard
Language
English · বাংলা

The Challenge

  • Data from multiple sources required manual integration and analysis.
  • No unified dashboard showed a clear picture of the dengue situation in Dhaka North at the ward level.
  • Geocoding of patient addresses to wards and zones was done manually, which was time-consuming.

What I Did

1. Automated data integration

  • Web-scraped hospital data from the DGHS dengue dashboard (daily aggregated values).
  • Collected individual patient and death records from the DNCC website (after verification).
  • Collected mosquito surveillance data (adult counts, BI, CI, HI) across five zones.

2. Automated geocoding

  • Used the Google Geocoding API to assign patient addresses to wards and zones (R code, run daily via GitHub Actions).

3. Data processing & analysis

  • Cleaned, integrated and analysed data in R, automated through GitHub Actions.

4. Dashboard development

  • Built the interactive dashboard in HTML and JavaScript, hosted on GitHub and linked from the DNCC website (Dengue Issue).

The Dengue Risk Index (DRI)

Built on a WHO-aligned Hazard–Exposure–Vulnerability framework; indicators are harmonised weekly, smoothed and min–max normalised.

HazardDengue patients · Case growth
ExposureMosquito density · BI, CI, HI
VulnerabilityPopulation density · Dengue deaths

DRI = (Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability)1/3: a ward ranks high only when all three are elevated together.

🟢 Low 0–33%
🟡 Medium 34–66%
🔴 High 67–100%

Impact

🎯
Identifies high-risk wards
🦟
Prioritises mosquito-control activities
📉
Monitors trends & improvement
⏱️
Reduces manual effort

Current status & next steps: the system provides automated surveillance and risk assessment, not yet predictive forecasting. Next: integrate rainfall and temperature, develop forecasting models, add health-system capacity indicators and strengthen early warning.

Key Features

  • Today's, weekly, monthly and yearly patient/death numbers with growth rates and year-on-year comparison
  • Daily, weekly, monthly and yearly trends (2022–present)
  • Interactive ward map with 3-level risk classification
  • Ward ranking, highest to lowest risk
  • Dengue prevention messages by risk level
  • Assistant health officer contacts by zone
  • Free dengue testing centres (ward-wise filter)
  • Bilingual: English & Bangla

  • Heat warnings reached people as numbers, with no plain guidance on how to protect themselves.
  • In an emergency, hospital beds, hospital locations and ambulance contacts weren't available in one place.
  • Weather data and emergency response information lived in separate places, so nobody could go from "it's dangerously hot" to "here's what to do and where to go" on one page.

What I Did

1. Automated weather data

  • Web-scraped Bangladesh Meteorological Department (BMD) data using R and GitHub Actions, updated every 3 hours.

2. One page for awareness and response

  • Brought weather, heatwave safety messages and hospital, bed and ambulance information together on the same page.

3. Data processing and storage

  • Processed and integrated the data in R, automated through GitHub Actions.
  • Used a Google Sheet as the database.

4. Dashboard development

  • Visualised the data in a web dashboard built with JavaScript and HTML.
  • Hosted it in a GitHub repository and linked it from a submenu of the DNCC website.

Impact

Faster access to emergency information

National COVID-19 Data Intelligence Platform

Participatory Surveillance & Early-Warning Intelligence for Bangladesh

Through telecom operators' hotline, USSD and app channels, people across Bangladesh self-reported COVID-19 symptoms by answering screening questions. I worked on the AI methods that used these answers to flag suspected and high-risk cases. Doctors verified them, and the verified high-risk list went to DGHS for sample collection and follow-up. The Challenge

  • Laboratory testing capacity was limited, and reporting delays, especially in rural and remote areas, made it hard to follow the outbreak in real time.
  • Hospitalisation and death data lag new infections by about two to three weeks.
  • Decision-makers needed earlier signals of where the outbreak was spreading and which people needed sample collection and follow-up.

What I Did

1. AI screening

  • Developed AI methods that analyse citizens' answers to screening questions, self-reported through telecom operators' channels (333, 16263, *3332#) and websites to flag people as suspected based on reported symptoms and as high risk for COVID-19.

2. Doctor verification and response

  • Doctors verified the flagged cases, and the verified high-risk patient list was sent to the Director General of Health Services (DGHS) for further monitoring, including sample collection and follow-up.

3. Further analysis (published)

  • The suspected and high-risk indicators were then aggregated by upazila and week and analysed against confirmed cases and mobility-based estimates, as reported in the published study.

4. Data curation and research outputs

  • Curated the self-reported data and co-authored the peer-reviewed article in Epidemics (data curation and original draft) and a UNDP policy brief.

What the Published Study Found

📞

🗺️

Linked to confirmed cases locally

In the study, the self-reported symptom and high-risk indicators were significantly correlated with lab-confirmed cases at the upazila level, in the same week and at one- and two-week lags. Multidimensional Poverty Lens for Urban Livelihood Support

Lessons from UNDP Bangladesh's LIUPC Project

A UNDP policy brief on whether a multidimensional approach to poverty works better in a crisis. It analyses survey data from 360 low-income urban households across seven cities to see how business grants and COVID-19 relief changed their multidimensional poverty scores.

Policy Research
Published
May 2022
Series
UNDP Development Futures Series
Policy brief
Read on UNDP.org

The Challenge

  • Bangladesh's official poverty analysis has focused on income, while Global MPI scores have consistently been higher than income-based estimates.
  • The COVID-19 crisis exposed the limits of a single, income-based measure, with estimates of 17.5 to 20 million new poor.
  • Programmes needed evidence on whether a multidimensional lens helps deliver support to the urban poor in a crisis.

My Contribution

Co-author, as a Research Officer at the UNDP Bangladesh Research Facility. My work covered:

  • Data processing
  • Statistical analysis and testing
  • Data visualisation
  • Writing

What the Study Did

1. The programme

  • Uses data from UNDP Bangladesh's Livelihoods Improvement of Urban Poor Communities (LIUPC) project, which uses the MPI to target cash grants for starting or expanding a business. Its database covers over 700,000 households.

2. Sampling

  • A stratified random sample of 360 households across seven cities (Chandpur, Chattogram, Dhaka North, Khulna, Mymensingh, Narayanganj and Sylhet), in three groups: vulnerable non-poor, poor grantees and poor non-grantees.

3. Analysis

  • Compares MPI scores before COVID-19 and about two years into the crisis (around February 2022), using descriptive and comparative analysis with ANOVA, paired t-tests and Welch two-sample t-tests at the 5% significance level.

Key Findings

📉
63% of poor grantee households reduced their MPI
⚠️
Vulnerable non-poor hit hardest (MPI up almost 13 points)
💵
Cash relief helped, including non-grantees
🧭
Gaps by disability, youth, gender and city

Business grants helped poor households improve their MPI scores. Vulnerable non-poor households, just above the programme's eligibility line, were the worst affected because they were largely left out of relief. Overall results also hid gaps: households with a member with a disability did worse, grantee households with youth beneficiaries improved the most, and Dhaka and Chattogram saw more progress than less developed cities.

Key Policy Messages

  • Adopt MPI-based programming to build resilience during crises
  • Consider the vulnerable non-poor in programme design and crisis response
  • Keep prioritising cash programmes and improve their efficiency
  • Invest in youth, other vulnerable groups and cities with fewer economic opportunities
  • Use new data and technology, such as big data, AI and mobile data, to track multidimensional poverty in real time

The Neglected Half: Female Youth Disengagement in Bangladesh

Labour Force Survey Analysis on Women's Economic Empowerment and NEET

A research paper on the disproportionate disengagement of young women in Bangladesh, measured through NEET (not in education, employment or training), and how COVID-19 affected it. I carried out the Labour Force Survey analysis behind it: participation and employment patterns, and an econometric model of what shapes women's economic status.

Policy Research
Data
Quarterly Labour Force Survey 2016–17
Tools
Stata
Theme
Women's economic empowerment

The Challenge

  • Women are about half of the population and half of the youth, yet the 2016–17 labour force survey shows NEET individuals are concentrated among youth (18–35) and are overwhelmingly female.
  • This underuse of women's potential is a missed chance to harness Bangladesh's demographic dividend.
  • Policymakers needed updated evidence on what drives inactivity among women, and on how COVID-19 made it worse.

What I Did

1. Data preparation

  • Processed the Quarterly Labour Force Survey (QLFS) 2016–17 microdata in Stata, applying survey weights. Recoded age, marital status, years of schooling, vocational training, urban/rural location, household size, relationship to the household head and the head's sex, and built an economic-status outcome: inactive, unemployed, salaried, self-employed or other employed.

2. Descriptive analysis

  • Estimated employment, unemployment and labour force participation (LFPR) by sex, age group, education, urban/rural area, division, district and marital status, along with average income and employment by occupation, status, sector and formal or informal job.

3. Econometric modelling

  • Built an econometric model (a multinomial logit) for women aged 15 and over, with inactivity as the reference group, to estimate how age, marriage, education, vocational training, location and household characteristics relate to women's economic status. This follows the outline's plan to repeat the reference study's econometric exercise with the 2016 survey.

Key Findings

🧭
36% women's labour force participation vs 80% for men
🚪
About 77% of people outside the labour force are women
🏘️
31% urban vs 39% rural female participation
🎓
Vocational training strongly linked to leaving inactivity

Among people aged 15 and over in the 2016–17 survey, labour force participation was about 58% overall but 36% for women against 80% for men, and women made up about 77% of those outside the labour force. Women's participation peaked at roughly 49% in the late 30s, was lower in urban (31%) than rural (39%) areas, and ranged from about 22% to 50% across divisions. In the econometric model, compared with being inactive, women with vocational training had much higher odds of being in every form of work or job search; urban women were more likely to be salaried and less likely to be self-employed; and married women were more likely to be self-employed or in other work but less likely to be salaried.

  • Implications of COVID-19 for female youth
  • The policy landscape and recommendations

A study using Bangladesh Peace Observatory data to examine how gender-based violence (GBV) changed before and after the onset of COVID-19: its trend, types, victims, perpetrators and where it concentrates. The Challenge

  • Violence against women and girls intensified behind the COVID-19 health emergency, in what has been called a shadow pandemic.
  • The study set out to understand the GBV trend before and after COVID-19, its nature, the main target and perpetrator groups, and its geographical distribution.
  • Regular, up-to-date data was needed, and the BPO database offers media-reported incident data from 2014.

My Contribution

  • Data filtering from the BPO website
  • Data processing
  • Analysis
  • Data visualisation

What the Study Did

1. Data source

  • Used the Bangladesh Peace Observatory (BPO) database, an online open-source record of violence incidents drawn from Bangla and English print and online media, with complete incident data from January 2014.

2. Filtering and extraction

  • Filtered the incidents from the BPO website for two periods, 1 May 2018 to 30 April 2020 (before COVID-19) and 1 May 2020 to 30 April 2021 (after its onset), keeping the Sexual and Gender-Based Violence category.

3. Processing and analysis

  • Adjusted divisional counts for population using BBS projections, checked the data for inconsistencies with R (none found), and cleaned and analysed it in R, with charts produced in Excel.

Key Findings

📈
Rising trend since May 2018, peaking around Sept–Oct
🆘
Sexual assault the leading type throughout
👧
Girls, women and young children about 90% of victims
🗺️
Barishal and Rajshahi emerge as hotspots

GBV incidents rose from May 2018 and peaked around September and October in both periods. Sexual assault was the leading type throughout, while domestic violence and dowry-related violence, which had been declining, rose after COVID-19 began. Girls, women and young children made up almost 90% of victims, and men were the largest perpetrator group, followed by youth and goon or miscreant groups. Barishal and Rajshahi emerged as hotspots at division level, and the top districts were also climate-vulnerable areas, a link the study suggests may be relevant. Because the data come from media reports, the true extent is likely higher.

Conclusions & Recommendations

  • BPO data reflect media-reported incidents only, so actual violence is likely higher
  • Regular household surveys are needed to complement it
  • Definitions of violence, victims and perpetrators need to be more consistent
  • More research is needed on the experiences of transgender and sexual minority communities
  • Programmes should reach poor, climate-vulnerable areas
